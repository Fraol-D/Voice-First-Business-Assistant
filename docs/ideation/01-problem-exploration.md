# Problem Exploration

This note records how the team moved from a broad search for a worthwhile problem to a narrower focus on **microbusiness operations**. It is an ideation record, not a claim that a product has been validated.

## Starting point

Ideation began without a fixed product. The search was for an operational problem that is frequent, costly when it fails, and poorly served by tools that assume office-style work: keyboards, forms, dashboards, and time set aside for “admin.”

Everyday economic activity in many places is still run by very small businesses: shops, kiosks, traders, service providers, and other owner-operated firms. These businesses generate a continuous stream of events — a sale, a restock, an expense, a customer taking goods on credit — while the owner is also serving customers, buying stock, and managing cash.

That combination made microbusiness operations a promising area to investigate, not because the team already had a solution, but because the work itself is information-heavy and often still handled by memory, paper, or tools that do not fit the work.

## Why operations, not a single feature

The interesting difficulty is not one isolated task. It is the chain from activity to decision:

1. **Capturing everyday business activity.** Sales, expenses, purchases, inventory changes, and customer debts happen in the flow of work. If capture is slow, awkward, or socially disruptive, it is skipped.
2. **Maintaining reliable business records.** Incomplete or delayed records make later numbers untrustworthy, even when the owner “knows” the business intuitively.
3. **Inventory awareness.** Without a current picture of what is on hand, restocking and selling on credit become guesses.
4. **Customer debts / receivables.** Informal credit is common. Who owes what, and whether a payment was received, is easy to lose when it lives in a notebook or in memory.
5. **Cash-flow awareness.** Money in, money out, and money still owed are different facts. Mixing them is a practical problem, not an accounting-theory problem.
6. **Turning records into operational decisions.** Even when some records exist, they often stay as a log. The owner still has to reconstruct answers such as what sold, what is running out, who has not paid, and whether the week was actually profitable.
7. **Barriers to conventional digital business tools.** Many existing products assume literacy with forms, menus, and reporting screens; reliable connectivity; and a willingness to adopt a new workflow. Research later in this process (see [02-research-findings.md](./02-research-findings.md)) describes digital-skill, usability, cost, and “no perceived need” barriers among microenterprises. Those findings did not select a product; they explained why a conventional app-first path might fail for the same operators the team was considering.

## What this investigation is not

This exploration does **not** prove that voice is the right interface, that microbusiness owners will adopt a new assistant, or that the product defined later is the solution.

It only explains why the team chose this problem area: the work already produces business events, those events are often poorly captured, and the gap between “what happened today” and “what should I do next” looks large enough to be worth studying.

The next step was to look at existing research rather than jump to a design.
